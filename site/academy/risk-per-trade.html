Position size and planned risk

The amount committed to an order and the amount at risk are different ideas.

← All lessons

A stop-based example

Suppose a hypothetical method defines a $20 planned loss and a stop 5% below entry. Ignoring fees and execution gaps, $20 ÷ 0.05 implies a $400 position. This is an explanation of stop-based sizing, not a recommendation to use those figures.

The original account-based example

In the source’s invented example, a $10,000 account has a planned risk fraction of 1%, or $100. Entry is $50 and the hypothetical stop is $46: an 8% distance. Position value is $100 ÷ 0.08 = $1,250, or 25 coins. Only if all 25 sell exactly $4 lower, with no costs, is the loss exactly $100.

This explains position value = current equity × planned risk fraction ÷ stop distance. It does not establish a NoGuru setting or an enforceable maximum loss.

Why the assumptions matter

A stop order is an execution instruction, not a guaranteed maximum loss. Gaps, slippage, outages and available liquidity can produce a worse fill. Without a stop, this formula cannot be used to claim that the position risks only $20.

The same ten losses, three fractions

Now assume each of ten consecutive trades loses exactly a fixed fraction of the remaining equity. There are no fees, gaps, deposits or other positions in this model. The 1%, 5% and 10% figures are source teaching examples, not suggested risk settings.

Hypothetical fractional-equity loss streak
Loss fraction each tradeEquity leftTotal lossGain needed to recover
1%90.4%−9.6%+10.6%
5%59.9%−40.1%+67.0%
10%34.9%−65.1%+186.8%

Equity left is (1 − fraction)10 of the start. The recovery gain is 1 ÷ equity-left fraction − 1. The original rounds the last recovery figure to 187%; 186.8% here is the same calculation to one decimal place. This table changes the size assumption, not the trade sequence or its probability.

Look beyond the first buy

Several additional buys can commit much more money than the opening order. Identify the total planned allocation, reserves, concurrent positions and exchange minimums before interpreting a small first order.

How this relates to NoGuru

NoGuru intentionally has no stop-loss. The generic 1–2% stop-risk idea in older educational material is not its strategy. The amount allocated can suffer substantial loss.
