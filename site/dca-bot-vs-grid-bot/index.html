DCA bot, grid bot or NoGuru’s Stepgrid: what is the difference?

Grid bots and DCA bots both buy as prices fall, and they are easy to mix up. Here is how each one works, when each earns and when each struggles, and where Stepgrid, the NoGuru bot, fits.

The short answer

A grid bot trades inside a price range you choose: it buys a little lower and sells a little higher, again and again, for as long as the price stays in that range.

A DCA bot follows one coin down: it buys more each time the price drops by a set amount, which lowers the average cost, and sells everything once the price is back above that average.

Stepgrid, the NoGuru bot, works in the DCA style on about 20 coins at once: it buys in steps as a coin falls, then sells above the average and follows the price up before it does. The settings are maintained for you and arrive as signals, so there is no range or spacing to configure.

What a grid bot does

You set a lowest price, a highest price and a number of steps. The bot places buy orders below the current price and sell orders above it, evenly spaced. Each time the price crosses a step and comes back, the bot has bought low and sold a step higher, and keeps the gap.

It earns most when a coin moves sideways and crosses the same steps many times. It struggles when the price leaves the range. Below the range, it is left holding everything it bought on the way down and stops trading. Above the range, it has sold out and takes no part in the rest of the rise.

So the result depends on choosing the range well, and on choosing it again when the market moves.

What a DCA bot does

In trading tools such as Cornix and 3Commas, “DCA bot” means this: the bot makes a first buy, then places further buys, often called safety orders, each time the price falls by a set percentage. Every extra buy lowers the average cost. When the price climbs a set percentage above that average, the bot sells the lot and starts again.

This is different from the everyday meaning of dollar-cost averaging, which is buying a fixed amount on a calendar, say every week, and never selling.

A DCA bot earns from dips that recover. It struggles in a long fall: once the last planned buy is used, it can only hold a coin that is below cost, unless you set a stop loss, which sells at a loss.

Where Stepgrid fits

Stepgrid is the strategy the NoGuru bot runs. It buys a coin in steps as it gets cheaper, up to a fixed limit per coin and a fixed total across all coins. Selling starts about 2% above the coin’s average cost; from there the bot follows the price up and sells when the rise runs out. A finished cycle is never sold at a loss, and there is no stop loss: a coin that keeps falling is held until its price recovers.

It watches about 20 established coins around the clock and decides, coin by coin, when to buy, how much, and when to sell. Coins are added and retired by rule, with a monthly review by a person.

It is spot only: you own the coins and nothing is borrowed. NoGuru publishes the instructions as signals, Cornix carries them out, and the money stays in your own exchange account.

Which one suits you

If you enjoy designing a strategy and adjusting it as the market changes, a grid or DCA bot you configure yourself gives you every control.

If you would rather run a maintained strategy and judge it by a public record, that is what Stepgrid is for. Its whole record is published: every finished trade, and the trades still open below cost.

One thing is the same for all three. They buy as prices fall, so in a long market fall each of them can be left holding coins worth less than they cost. None of them removes that risk.
