Price-staged buying and calendar DCA

Calendar dollar-cost averaging buys on a schedule, such as each month. This strategy plans purchase levels around price movement. A later purchase happens only if its level and the strategy’s conditions are met.

Both approaches spread buying across time, but they answer different questions: when the calendar says to buy, or when price reaches a planned level. Neither removes the risk that the coin keeps falling.

Why the average is weighted by quantity

Average purchase cost is the total spent divided by the total units bought. Buying more units at a lower price can reduce the average cost of that same cycle. It also increases the amount exposed to the coin.

The example is arithmetic before fees, not a trade recommendation or operating allocation. Lowering the average does not guarantee a sale or recovery.

Spend 100 USDT at a price of 100: buy 1 unit. Spend another 100 USDT at a price of 80: buy 1.25 units. Total cost is 200 USDT for 2.25 units. The weighted average is 200 ÷ 2.25 = 88.89 USDT per unit, rounded.

A new lower cycle has its own average

If the rules and available cash allow, a smaller separate cycle may begin lower while an older one waits. Each cycle has its own purchases and average. A new cycle does not rewrite the old one.

A combined average across every held unit of the same coin is a different calculation. It does not mean that separate cycles have been merged or that an older exit target has changed.

Illustrative same-coin holdings: cycle A has 1 unit bought at 100; cycle B has 1 unit bought at 60. A’s average remains 100, and B’s is 60. The combined holding average is 80. Those are three different measurements.

Cash gives a later purchase room to happen

Reserve cash supports planned purchases that have not filled yet. Purchases commit it; completed sales may make capital available again. The reserve varies, and it can run low.

Sizing and spacing affect how quickly capital becomes committed. The strategy has finite capacity: an attractive level does not create cash. More capital can support purchases, but it does not guarantee higher percentage returns.

Completed results and holdings still open

A completed cycle has sold its holdings. Its trading result depends on its purchase and sale prices and the costs included in the calculation. A public before-fee figure must not be described as net profit after all costs.

Whole-account value is cash plus the current market value of holdings. Completed gains are already inside that value. Adding them again would count them twice. Holdings still open can reduce account value even while completed cycles show gains.

The current public signal feed shows completed cycles and open aggregates. It does not provide the cash and monetary holdings needed to reconcile a current whole account.

Account value = cash + held units valued at current prices. Completed profit helps explain how that value changed; it is not another amount to add on top.

Waiting costs capacity as well as time

A cycle below its exit conditions may keep holding the coin. That capital is not available for another purchase. If much of the allocation is committed, there may be little new buying even when prices move.

The holding can lose market value or never recover. There is no strategy stop-loss. Pausing new trades does not close existing holdings or open orders; those need a separate decision.

A coin can receive less future allocation or stop receiving new purchases. That does not itself sell existing holdings or release their capital. A completed sale may be followed by another cycle in the same coin only while approval, rules and cash allow.
